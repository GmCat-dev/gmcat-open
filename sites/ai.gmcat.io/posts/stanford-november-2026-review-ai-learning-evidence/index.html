AI and Learning: What 52 Causal Studies Show So Far

A Stanford review published 10 November 2026 gathers the strongest experimental evidence on generative AI in education. Some uses help, some backfire, and design matters a lot.

A reality check on AI for learning

Claims about AI tutors are everywhere. Good evidence is rarer. On 10 November 2026, Stanford's SCALE Initiative published an updated review of the causal evidence on generative AI in education. Causal evidence here means randomized trials and strong quasi-experiments. The authors are Seth Walker, Lily Fesler, JP Martinez Claeys, Chris Agnew and Susanna Loeb.

How the review was built

The team screened 868 papers published between October 2025 and June 2026. Only 32 met its bar for strong causal evidence. Added to an earlier March review, that makes 52 studies, most of them about tools students use directly.

What the evidence suggests

Letting AI do the work can weaken learning. In several trials, students who used AI to produce their work did worse later when tested without it. In one study, novice programmers coded better with ChatGPT, but their understanding did not improve.

Tutoring results are mixed. Some studies found gains. One reported a 0.258 standard-deviation math improvement in Sierra Leone. Others found no added benefit.

A large US trial shows how mixed the picture can be. Oreopoulos, Liut, Sungu and Low randomized more than 6,000 middle-school students on a math practice platform. Students with AI support moved more slowly and attempted fewer questions, but they were more accurate when they did. The most encouraging result came from combining AI help with a work-until-mastery approach. Even then, the gains on a test a week later were only marginally significant and limited to material the students had practiced.

Feeling helped is not the same as learning. The review notes that tools can reduce workload or feel clearer without improving performance once the AI is removed.

Teacher tools can cut both ways. In one trial in Turkey, giving teachers a broad AI teaching tool lowered student motivation and confidence, with no clear change in test scores. Other uses look more promising. In a randomized trial with 354 economics students, teaching assistants got AI suggestions for essay feedback and could accept, edit or reject them. Students who received this AI-assisted feedback wrote noticeably better revisions, and the gains grew as assistants used more of the suggestions.

What this means for ordinary learners and teachers

The pattern is consistent. AI seems to help more when it supports a person's own thinking or a teacher's judgment, and less when it replaces the effort that produces learning. The review says design and delivery matter, but which specific features matter is still unclear.

Limitations

  • Mostly preprints. The review's research repository is mostly arXiv preprints that haven't been peer reviewed, though at least two human researchers reviewed each causal paper. The FeedbackWriter trial above is also a preprint, and the math trial is an NBER working paper, which is not peer reviewed either.
  • Short horizons. The authors say few studies measure learning long after AI access ends. Most follow-ups last days or weeks.
  • Who was studied. Most new studies come from colleges, often in science and computer science. The review offers little new evidence on equity and warns against generalizing to US K-12 schools.
  • Possible gaps. The search relied mainly on arXiv, so it may have missed journal publications.

Bottom line

The evidence does not support big promises about AI transforming learning. Nor does it show AI is useless. It points to a narrower claim: carefully designed AI help, used alongside human effort, sometimes improves learning. The details matter.
