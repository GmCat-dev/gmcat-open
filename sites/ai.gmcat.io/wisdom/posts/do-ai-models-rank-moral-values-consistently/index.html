Do AI Models Rank Moral Values Consistently? A New Test Finds Mostly Not

A new preprint checks whether five large language models make internally consistent choices when moral values conflict. Most of the time they don't, and averaging their answers can hide that.

The question

AI companies often show that a chatbot scores well on a morality benchmark. A good score shows the answers look right. It doesn't show how the model got there. A 2026 Nature Perspective by Haas and colleagues separates two things: moral performance, meaning appropriate-looking outputs, and moral competence, meaning outputs that come from weighing the relevant considerations.

A new preprint tests one basic requirement for that kind of weighing.

What the researchers did

In a paper submitted to arXiv on 6 November 2026, Wasson, Song and colleagues ask whether a model's moral choices are transitive. If a model treats violating A as worse than violating B, and B as worse than C, it should also treat A as worse than C. Without this, the model has no consistent ranking of values.

They tested five models in non-reasoning modes: Llama 4 Maverick, GPT-5.2, Gemini 2.5 Flash, DeepSeek V3.2 and Grok 4.1 Fast. Each model chose between pairs of short scenarios that set seven moral values against each other: physical care, emotional care, fairness, liberty, loyalty, authority and sanctity. Every pairing appeared at six severity levels and in both presentation orders, which gave 60 sets of choices in all.

The team did not just count answers. It read the probabilities each model gave its options and fitted three statistical models of consistent choice, ranging from lenient to strict.

What they found

  • Consistency was patchy. The most lenient test of a consistent ranking held in 36 of the 60 sets. The strictest held in only 8. A middle option, which allows a model to mix several consistent rankings, held in 13.
  • Order mattered. When the researchers swapped which scenario came first, the ranking of values changed in 26 of 30 cases.
  • Averaging hid the problem. After pooling each model's answers, four of the five models looked consistent, and all four put physical care first and loyalty last. That tidy ranking did not match any of the individual sets. The authors argue it is an artifact of averaging.

The authors conclude that morality evaluations should first check whether a model's choices have any structure before asking whether that structure matches human values.

Why it matters

This connects to a wider push to test how models reason, not just what they conclude. MoReBench, presented at ICLR 2026, scores the reasoning process against expert-written rubrics. Its authors found that a model's scale and its math, coding and science scores did not predict its moral reasoning ability. Both studies point the same way: polished answers can hide shallow or unstable reasoning.

Limitations and skeptical views

  • Preprint. The paper has not been peer reviewed.
  • Narrow setup. The authors note that they measured the model's first-token preference before any reasoning, tested only non-reasoning configurations, used one moral framework and one prompt design, and relied on scenario ratings validated with humans, not models. Reasoning modes might behave differently.
  • Necessary, not sufficient. The authors say transitivity alone does not prove moral competence.
  • Noisy choices are hard to read. Research on human decisions warns that claims of intransitive preferences need very strong evidence, because inconsistent-looking data can come from a mix of consistent rankings. The study's mixture model tries to address this.
  • Is a fixed ranking even wise? Haas and colleagues stress that moral decisions depend on many context-sensitive factors. Some shifting across severity levels could reflect sensitivity to context rather than incoherence. Sensitivity to the order a question is asked in, however, is harder to defend.

The takeaway

This is a careful early test, not a verdict on AI morality. It shows that a single benchmark score, or an average across many prompts, can make a model look more principled than it is.
